Evidence · Invertebrates

Two stations, one method, one river — and only one of them has a score

The Environment Agency samples river invertebrates at Teddington and again four kilometres downstream at Ham. Both sit in the same tidal water body. Both are worked with the same three-minute pond net. The Teddington samples arrive with a full suite of biological indices attached. The Ham samples arrive with none — not one index value across a hundred and two samples and twenty-one unbroken years. The difference is not ecological. It is which of two download families the station was filed under.

The split, and why it is administrative rather than ecological

Anyone downloading “the invertebrate data” for this reach takes one file and sees half the river. The long modern record at Teddington and the long modern record at Ham are in two different downloads, and they behave differently once you open them.

The freshwater family (INV_OPEN_DATA) ships three files — SITE, METRICS and TAXA. The marine benthic family (TC_BENT_OPEN_DATA_TAXA) ships one: taxa. There is no site file and no metrics file. That is the whole of the gap, and it is narrower and more fixable than “the tidal data is not published”.

What makes the division hard to defend on ecological grounds is that it does not follow the water, the salinity or the method. It cuts across all three, in both directions at once:

  • The “freshwater” Teddington station is coded as an estuary. Site 98142 carries WATERBODY_TYPE = WBES, which the EA’s own lookup expands to “ESTUARY: Lower section of a river between freshwater limit and the sea”, and it sits in tidal water body GB530603911403. It is in the freshwater download all the same — along with four further WBES-coded sites in the same water body.
  • The “marine” Ham station is worked with a freshwater net. Of the rows at site 142308, 2,191 are S3PO — the EA’s “3-MIN POND NET (BT001): 3-min active sampling, 1-min hand search”, the standard freshwater river method — against 17 rows of marine kick sampling. It is the same method used at Teddington, where it produces a BMWP score.
  • So the identical method, in the identical water body, produces a full index suite at one station and nothing at the other. The only thing that differs is the download family. That is a filing decision, not a scientific one.

We identify the Ham station by coordinate alone — easting 516981, northing 173208. Because no dataset carries a name, we will not print the word “Ham” against that ID as though it were official. It is our label for a grid reference, and we have asked the Environment Agency to confirm it.

What a scored station looks like — Teddington, 1983–2026

This is the series Ham does not have. It is shown to make the gap concrete, not to argue a trend: across forty-three years the Average Score Per Taxon moves between roughly 4.1 and 5.2 with no clear direction, and the record has a fourteen-year hole in the middle.

The most recent sample, 16 June 2026, returned a BMWP total of 41 from 9 scoring families, an ASPT of 4.56. Mean ASPT for 2014–2026 is 4.74 against 4.27 for the three years available before 1990 — but three years is not a baseline, and we do not present that difference as an improvement.

The ask, stated narrowly

This is not a demand for new monitoring. The samples exist, the taxa are published, and the indices are already calculated for identically-sampled stations a few kilometres upstream. Three things would close the gap:

  • A metrics extract for the marine benthic family, or a statement that no index is calculated for these samples because the freshwater tools are not valid below 18 PSU. Either answer is useful. The absence of both is what makes the data unusable.
  • Station names, or a published ID-to-name lookup. The TraC fish download carries SITE_NAME, LOCATION_NAME and SITE_PARENT_NAME. The invertebrate downloads carry none, which means no member of the public can tell that a number is the river at their own bridge.
  • A note on the family boundary — which stations are filed where, and on what basis — so that anyone joining the two does not silently lose half the reach.

These have been put to the Environment Agency’s marine team directly. This page will record the answer, including if the answer is that no index can properly be calculated.

How to read this

Caveats a regulator would press — stated up front

  • The absence of an index is not evidence of poor water. It is evidence that the published data does not let anyone say either way. Nothing on this page claims the Ham reach is in bad condition; the claim is that its condition is not derivable from what is published.
  • There may be a good scientific reason. BMWP, WHPT and their relatives are freshwater tools and are not considered valid in brackish water — the EA has made this point to us directly. If that is why no index ships, it is a defensible position and we would publish it as the answer. It is simply not currently stated anywhere in the downloads.
  • Our station labels are inferences from grid references. No invertebrate dataset carries names. “Ham”, “Teddington” and the rest are our descriptions, unconfirmed by the EA at the time of writing.
  • The Teddington series has a fourteen-year hole. No samples are published between 1990 and 2004. Annual means either side of that gap are not a continuous record, and the three pre-1990 years rest on 17 samples between them.
  • Annual means hide within-year variation. Sample counts range from 1 to 15 per year. A year represented by a single autumn sample is not comparable with a year of fifteen; the tooltip on each bar gives the sample count.
  • Method changes matter. Site 137942 mixes marine kick sampling with pond net across its 37 years, and the pre-1994 marine methods (kick, gulley dredge, core, quadrat) are not comparable with post-2005 pond netting. We do not chart that station as one line.
  • This is a data-architecture finding, not a pollution finding. It belongs to the programme’s question of whether the official evidence base is usable, and should not be cited as evidence about the state of the river.
