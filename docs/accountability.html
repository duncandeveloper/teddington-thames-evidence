Fines, penalties & accountability

The regulatory record on Thames Water — the operator of the sewage works and storm overflows on this reach. In 2024–2025 the company drew the largest financial penalty ever imposed on a water company, the most pollution incidents of any company, and the lowest environmental rating the Environment Agency gives. Every figure here is a proven penalty or a published official count; where cases are still before the courts, they are alleged, not decided.

The headline figures

Two regulators sit over Thames Water: Ofwat (the economic regulator, which sets prices and enforces the operating licence) and the Environment Agency (which rates environmental performance and brings pollution prosecutions in the courts). In 2024–2025 both recorded the same thing — documented failure — while the company continued to pay dividends and award staff bonuses.

Ofwat's record £122.7m penalty

On 28 May 2025 Ofwat imposed a £122.7 million regulatory penalty on Thames Water following two investigations — the largest financial penalty ever placed on a water company. It has two distinct parts.

The larger part — £104.5m — was for failures in how Thames Water managed its sewage-treatment works and wastewater network. The second part — £18.2m — is unusual: Ofwat found the company had paid dividends in October 2023 and March 2024 that breached the conditions of its operating licence, because the payments did not properly reflect the company's performance and service to customers.

Dividends & bonuses — the context

The dividend penalty is one strand of a wider debate about money leaving a company that was, at the same time, in financial distress and failing environmentally. The verified points:

  • Dividends. Ofwat first found provisionally (Dec 2024) that the October 2023 and March 2024 dividends breached licence condition P30, proposing an £18m penalty; the finding was confirmed at £18.2m on 28 May 2025. Separately, Ofwat said it would recover £131.3m of associated tax benefits from the company through price controls. PUBLISHED (Ofwat; Water Magazine, Dec 2024)
  • Bonuses (Apr 2025). Thames Water paid about £2.5m of "retention" bonuses to 21 senior managers (part of a £15.7m plan), funded from its £3bn emergency loan, and declined to claw them back despite government objection; Ofwat said it had not been consulted before the payments were made. PUBLISHED (ITV News, Jul 2025)
  • Bonuses blocked (Jun 2025). Using new powers under the Water (Special Measures) Act 2025, Ofwat blocked more than £4m of executive bonuses across six water companies — Thames Water among them — whose pay failed to reflect environmental performance. PUBLISHED (BBC News, Jun 2025)

Bonus and dividend decisions are lawful corporate choices reported in the public record; only the two dividends above were found by Ofwat to breach the licence. They are included here as context, not as further findings of wrongdoing.

2024: the worst pollution record of any water company

The Environment Agency's annual Environmental Performance Report rates each company on pollution incidents and compliance. For 2024 it recorded Thames Water's 523 pollution incidents — more than any other company — and downgraded it to a bottom 1-star rating, the only company at that level. The sharpest rise was in the most serious incidents.

Criminal fines in the courts

Separate from Ofwat's regulatory penalties, the Environment Agency prosecutes water companies in the criminal courts, where a conviction can bring a fine. These fines are proven outcomes — but the most widely quoted cumulative figure for Thames Water is now out of date.

  • £24.4m — a Feb-2021 snapshot, not a current total. The often-cited "£24.4m" was the Environment Agency's cumulative total across nine cases (in Oxfordshire, Berkshire and Buckinghamshire), quoted when Thames Water was fined £2.3m over the Henley/Fawley pollution in February 2021. It is real, but it is a five-year-old figure. PUBLISHED (Big Issue, quoting the EA)
  • Later proven fines push the true total higher. Since that snapshot, further convictions include a £4m fine (Oxford, Nov 2021) and a £3.3m fine (Crawley / Gatwick catchment, Jul 2023), so the genuine cumulative total is now £30m+. DERIVED — the exact current cumulative figure needs a single quoted source and should not be stated to the penny.

Do not present "£24.4m" as the company's current criminal-fines total. It understates the position; use it only as a February-2021 figure, and treat the £30m+ total as an estimate pending a quoted post-2023 source.

How to read this

Fairness & legal care — stated up front

  • Proven vs alleged. Ofwat's £122.7m is a regulatory penalty that has been imposed; the Environment Agency's court fines are criminal convictions. Both are proven. Where a prosecution or investigation is still ongoing, the matter is alleged and unproven until a court decides — this page states only outcomes that have actually been reached, and does not attribute unproven allegations to any named individual.
  • The £24.4m is outdated. It is a February-2021 snapshot of nine cases, not a current total; later fines make the real cumulative figure higher (£30m+). Don't read it as today's number.
  • Keep the 2024 percentages distinct. +~33% (total incidents), +135% (serious), +34% (sewerage-only) and +29% (whole sector) measure different things and are routinely confused. The counts — 523 total, 33 serious, 1-star — are the firm figures.
  • These are company-wide figures. The 523 incidents and the star rating cover Thames Water's entire region, not specifically the Teddington–Richmond reach. They describe the operator and its regulatory record, not this single stretch of river.
  • Bonuses and dividends are context. They are reported facts about lawful corporate decisions that drew government and regulator objection; only the two dividends Ofwat named were found to breach the licence. Nothing here implies illegality beyond the proven penalties.
  • A fine is not a river measurement. A penalty records a legal or regulatory failure; it does not quantify how much pollution reached the Thames. No spill volume or pollutant load is published anywhere (see the sewage & storm-overflows page) — so fines speak to accountability, not to a measured quantity of harm.
