Awareness · Why it matters

A living river, and a water industry in crisis.

The tidal Thames was declared biologically dead in 1957. Today it is home to seals, seahorses and more than 115 species of fish. That recovery is real — and it is now under strain. The company that runs the sewage works on this reach drew the largest penalty ever placed on a water company, recorded the most pollution incidents of any company, and the regulator itself is being abolished. This page sets out, in plain terms, why the evidence matters — and, just as plainly, where the evidence stops.

The problem, plainly

Why this reach, why now

Between Teddington Weir and Richmond, the Thames is a place people row, walk, watch birds and — as of 2026 — officially swim. It is also where a very large sewage works discharges to the river, where two water bodies have sat below “good” health for three planning cycles, and where the operator has been penalised at record scale. Those four numbers point the same way: documented environmental pressure on this reach, and a documented record of corporate and regulatory failure around it. But a fair reading needs the other half of the picture too. The tidal river is, on the long view, recovering — dissolved oxygen has been improving since 2007 (ZSL, statistically significant), and the freshwater stretch above the weir stays well-oxygenated. Much of the tidal biology — fish, plankton, invertebrates — scores Good or High. The honest case is not “a dead river”; it is a river under pressure, run by a system that is visibly struggling to look after it. Where the data cannot prove a link, this page says so — see the limits of the evidence at the foot of the page.

The national picture · accountability

A national reckoning over water — stated with legal care

This reach does not sit in isolation. Over five years, the water industry has moved from record criminal fines and a record regulatory penalty, through a new law that can jail executives, to the abolition of the economic regulator itself. The account below keeps two lines firmly apart: a regulatory penalty (imposed by Ofwat) and a criminal conviction (a fine handed down by a court) are both proven; a charge or an investigation is an allegation, unproven until a court decides.

What is proven

  • Ofwat's record £122.7m penalty on Thames Water (28 May 2025) — the largest ever on a water company: £104.5m for wastewater-operations failures and £18.2m for paying dividends that breached its licence. A regulatory penalty, imposed. PUBLISHED
  • Southern Water fined a then-record £90m (July 2021) after pleading guilty to 6,971 illegal discharges from 17 sites (2010–2015); the judge found the offending deliberate. A criminal conviction. PUBLISHED
  • Thames Water criminal fines include £4m (Oxford, Nov 2021), £3.3m (Gatwick catchment, Jul 2023) and £2.3m (Henley, Feb 2021) — each after a guilty plea. Convictions, proven. PUBLISHED
  • Thames Water's 2024 record: 523 pollution incidents (most of any company), 33 of them serious (up 135%), and a bottom 1-star EA rating — the only company at 1-star. Published counts. PUBLISHED
  • The Water (Special Measures) Act 2025 received Royal Assent (Feb 2025) and came into force; it allows executives to face up to two years in prison for obstructing investigations or concealing illegal discharges, and lets Ofwat block bonuses. PUBLISHED

What is alleged, proposed or still unfolding

  • Charged, not convicted. In July 2026 the Environment Agency charged a former Southern Water chief executive and three ex-colleagues with conspiracy to defraud, alleging manipulation of self-monitoring between 2012 and 2017. These are charges only; all defendants are presumed innocent unless and until convicted. PUBLISHED (report of a charge)
  • A guilty plea, awaiting sentence. South West Water pleaded guilty (Mar 2026) to 18 charges covering 2015–2021; sentencing was listed for 30 July 2026. Guilt admitted; the sentence is a court matter. PUBLISHED
  • Ongoing investigations. A major EA/Ofwat investigation into 2,000+ sewage treatment works (opened 2021) was later widened to every English water and sewerage company. Investigations are not findings of guilt. PUBLISHED
  • The regulator itself. Following the Cunliffe review, the Government confirmed (Jul 2025) that Ofwat will be abolished and replaced by a single stronger regulator; the NAO and the Public Accounts Committee had found regulators “failed to deliver a trusted and resilient water sector.” PUBLISHED

Wording matters here. “Charged” and “alleged” are never presented as “convicted.” Regulatory penalties (Ofwat) are distinct from criminal convictions (the courts). Where a fine was later reduced on appeal — as with Welsh Water's £1.35m (2025) — that is noted in the feed. Bonus and dividend decisions are lawful corporate choices reported in the public record; only the two dividends Ofwat named were found to breach a licence.

The limits of the evidence — read this before you share a number

What the data does not prove (stated up front)

  • No spill volume is published — anywhere. Monitoring records how long and how often a storm overflow discharges, never how much. There is no open measure of how much sewage enters the tidal Thames here.
  • The worst water-quality readings are historical. The lowest recorded dissolved-oxygen levels are from 2004–2006; recent tidal oxygen has improved. Do not read a present-day crisis from a twenty-year-old minimum.
  • Spill hours are volatile, not a clean upward trend. They track rainfall. Mogden's discharge fell from 471.7 hours in 2024 to 202.5 hours in 2025 — most likely a drier year, not proven investment. Avoid “worse every year.”
  • The chemical “Fail” is largely a national artefact. The river's failing chemical status is driven mainly by ubiquitous persistent chemicals (PBDEs, PFOS, mercury) that fail in almost all English waters — not solely by Thames Water.
  • The tidal biology is largely healthy. Fish, plankton and invertebrates score Good or High; the “Moderate” classification is about chemistry and physical modification, not dead biology.
  • Wildlife record counts reflect effort, not population. More sightings usually means more recording. Biodiversity records are context, not proof that any decline was caused by the water company.
  • Correlation is not causation. The link between spills and oxygen sags is inferred from seasonal timing, not yet demonstrated by overlaying spill dates on continuous oxygen data. It is an open question, flagged as such.
  • Zinc is unmonitored. Zinc is a named driver of the tidal body's status, yet it has zero records at any of the local water-quality points — so that classification cannot be checked from open data.
  • Company-wide figures describe the operator, not this reach. The 523 incidents and the 1-star rating cover Thames Water's whole region; they speak to the operator's record, not specifically to the water between Teddington and Richmond.
  • A fine is not a river measurement. Penalties record legal or regulatory failure; they do not quantify how much pollution reached the river.
